Introduction

This artifact is a newsletter I researched, wrote, and designed for AIML-500 Machine Learning Foundations. It looks at four commercial AI developments released between July 21 and August 13, 2026, and focuses on what they actually change for businesses, not just what the technology can do. I wrote it for readers who may be interested in AI but do not have a technical background. The newsletter itself is the artifact, presented as a standalone webpage in this portfolio.

Objective

The assignment was to find recent commercial AI applications across three different industries and look at how they affect business processes and decision-making. The research was only part of the challenge. I also had to explain things like pricing, clinical results, and vendor-reported metrics in a way that would make sense to someone without an AI background.

That meant I could not simply repeat what companies said in their product announcements. I had to look at the numbers more closely, understand what they actually measured, and explain the limitations along with the benefits.

Project Overview

I chose technology, healthcare, and retail because they show very different ways AI is being used commercially. Technology focuses on the tools businesses build with, healthcare involves a much higher-stakes decision-making environment, and retail shows how AI can change a familiar customer experience.

The products I covered were Claude Opus 5, Gemini 3.7 Flash, OpenAI Presence, Perimeter's Claire OCT System, and Michaels' Ask Mike. Together, they gave me examples ranging from AI model pricing and enterprise agents to surgical imaging and conversational shopping.

One thing I wanted to avoid was making every product sound like a success story. For each section, I looked at both the benefit and the trade-off. I asked four basic questions: What was released? What business process does it change? What do the reported numbers actually tell us? And what questions are still unanswered?

For example, Claire's reported sensitivity increased from 48.7% to 61.5%, but specificity decreased from 72.1% to 65.8%. That makes the result more interesting than simply saying the system became “more accurate.” Similarly, Michaels' conversion numbers came from shoppers who chose to use the assistant, so that group may already have had stronger purchase intent than other shoppers.

Process

This was an individual project. My process was fairly straightforward:

  • I started with the assignment rubric and defined a three-week research window so that every product I covered had actually been released during the required period.
  • I researched potential releases and narrowed them down to three industries that showed noticeably different uses of AI.
  • I checked important claims against primary sources whenever possible, including the FDA device record for Claire's clinical results and vendor sources for model pricing.
  • I wrote the initial analysis and then went back through it with a non-technical reader in mind. The goal was to simplify the language without removing important numbers or caveats.
  • I built the final newsletter as a responsive HTML page with a print stylesheet and included it in this portfolio as a standalone artifact.

Value Proposition

The main value of this project is that it pushed me to look at AI products from a business perspective instead of just a technical one. For each product, I tried to identify the actual workflow being changed, understand what the reported results did and did not prove, and consider what a company would be giving up by adopting the technology.

That last part became especially important during the research. A product can have impressive benchmark results or a lower price and still be a poor fit for a particular organization. The real question is whether it solves a meaningful problem and can be introduced into an existing workflow without creating bigger problems somewhere else.

The project also gave me practice translating technical information for a general audience. Terms such as sensitivity, specificity, and per-token pricing are useful to specialists but can be confusing to everyone else. Learning how to explain those ideas without oversimplifying them is something I can apply well beyond this particular newsletter.

Key Learnings

The biggest lesson I took from this project was to be more skeptical of favorable numbers. Almost every product announcement came with an impressive metric, but the context around the metric was often more interesting.

Claire's higher sensitivity came with lower specificity. Michaels' reported conversion results were based on users who voluntarily tried the assistant, which makes the comparison less straightforward. And reading the FDA information instead of relying only on the company's announcement gave me a more complete picture of what Claire was actually approved and intended to do.

I also came away with a stronger sense that AI capability is only one part of the adoption decision. Across the examples I looked at, workflow fit mattered just as much. Someone has to be able to use the system effectively, catch mistakes when they matter, and remain accountable for important decisions. A lower token price is useful, but it does not automatically make a product more valuable.
