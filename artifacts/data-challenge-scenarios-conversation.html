Scenario 1: Missing Data

You're building a model to predict customer churn for a telecom company. Your dataset has 50,000 records with features like account age, monthly charges, and service usage. You discover that 15% of the records have missing values in the "customer service calls" column — some are completely empty, others have clearly placeholder values like "-1" or "999."

How would you handle this missing data before training your model? Walk me through your approach and the reasoning behind each decision.

Me

I would first identify all missing values, including empty cells and placeholder values such as -1 or 999, and convert these placeholders to standard missing values. I would then check if the missing values have some patterns, i.e. related to some other features in the data. If the missing values are random, I would impute the values. Since 15% of the records have missing values, I don't want to delete those data directly, which would lead to a data loss. I'm not really sure about the situation if the missing values have a specific pattern. I feel it may be a good idea to create an indicator for the missing values, i.e. the original value is missing or not, which may also contribute to predicting customer churn.

SchoolAI

Solid thinking here. You're right to avoid dropping 15% of your data — that's substantial loss, and deletion can also introduce bias if the missingness isn't random.

A few things to dig into:

Your indicator variable idea is strong — if "customer service calls" is missing, that could itself be meaningful. Customers who never call might behave differently from those who call frequently. Creating a binary flag for missingness is a smart move.

On imputation: You mentioned you'd impute, but how would you choose the method? Mean/median? Something more sophisticated? What would guide that decision?

On patterned missingness: You're unsure what to do if missing values follow a pattern. Let me push you: if you discovered that customers with newer accounts were more likely to have missing service call data, how would that change your approach? What does that pattern tell you about the data itself?

Pick one of these threads — which one feels like the biggest gap in your thinking right now?

Me

I feel the biggest gap in my thinking for now is what to do when the missing values have a specific pattern instead of being random. If customers with new accounts were more likely to have missing service call data, this means the missing values are not random anymore. My first thought is that might be some data collection issue. I would probably check and fix the data quality problem first rather than imputing values. Apart from that, in that case, the missing pattern itself may contain useful information for prediction, I would lean on creating a missing-value indicator instead of using direct imputation.

SchoolAI

You're spot on — this is exactly the right instinct. When missingness follows a pattern, it's often Missing Not At Random (MNAR), and that's fundamentally different from random missingness.

Your diagnosis is sharp: newer accounts missing service call data could mean the data collection system wasn't capturing calls for new customers initially, or there's a reporting lag. That's a data quality issue you'd want to investigate at the source.

Here's where I want to push your thinking further:

If the missingness pattern is informative — say, customers with zero calls are systematically having their data dropped — and you create an indicator variable, what happens when you train your model? What does that indicator actually capture?

And more importantly: if you impute and include the indicator, how might those two features interact? Would the model learn something useful, or could you be introducing redundancy or confusion?

Think about what the model would actually see during inference — what would a new customer with truly unknown service call data look like to your model?

Me

Yeah, if customers with zero service calls are systematically recorded as missing, the indicator may become a strong factor related to customer behavior rather than data quality. It could just indirectly represent customers who have no service calls.

After imputation, the model will see two factors: the imputed customer service calls and the missing-value indicator. The imputed value serves as a numerical input, but the indicator tells the model if the original value is missing or not. I don't think these two factors are redundant because they give different information. Two customers can have the same customer service calls but one is from imputation and the other one is from the real data.

During inference, if a new customer has an unknown service call value, I would apply the same imputation as the other training data and set the missing-value indicator as true.

SchoolAI

You're thinking clearly about this. The distinction you're making is important — the imputed value gives you a best guess at what the feature should be, while the indicator tells you something about how confident that guess is (or what the missingness itself signals).

Here's one more nuance to consider:

What if the model learns to rely heavily on the indicator? If "missing" is strongly predictive of churn, the model might essentially ignore the imputed service call value and just use the indicator as a proxy. That could work fine if the missingness pattern stays consistent. But what happens if the data collection process changes — say, the company fixes the bug and now new accounts do capture service calls? Suddenly your indicator becomes useless, and the model's learned dependency on it could hurt performance.

How would you guard against that? Or would you accept it as a limitation and monitor for drift?

That's a subtle point, but worth thinking about. Overall though — you've got a solid approach here. You're thinking about the why behind missingness, not just mechanically applying techniques.

That's actually a good point. I think this is a trade-off between performance and robustness. The only way I could think of to guard against that is continuously monitoring the missing pattern and the model performance. If the number of missing values drop a lot, we may need to consider retraining the model with the new data. Besides, I would compare the model performance with and without the missing-value indicator. If the indicator cannot improve the model performance anymore, we may consider dropping it.

SchoolAI

That's exactly the right mindset. Monitoring and retraining are your safeguards here. The A/B comparison is also smart — sometimes you discover the indicator isn't adding much value, and simpler is better.

My intuition is to avoid imputation with mean because the data is right-skewed. I would probably start with median imputation plus the missing-value indicator I mentioned.

Good instinct — you're right that mean imputation would be problematic here. With a right-skewed distribution, the mean gets pulled toward the tail and wouldn't represent what most customers actually experience.

  • Mode imputation (most frequent value)?
  • Using a model-based approach — predicting the missing service call values based on other features like account age, monthly charges, or usage patterns?
  • Creating bins or categories instead of imputing a raw number?

What would make you choose median over these alternatives? And what would make you consider something more sophisticated?

Me

Mode could also work especially if most customers have 0 or 1 calls. As for the model-based approach, we'll need to check the correlation between the customer service calls and the other features. If there's a strong relationship, I'll consider model-based imputation. SchoolAI

You're weighing the trade-offs well. Let me push you on the decision-making itself:

For median vs mode, we should look into the distribution of the feature. For model-based imputation, you're right, correlation should not be the only standard. Overall though — you've got a clear approach now:

Scenario 2: Imbalanced Datasets

You're building a fraud detection system for a credit card company. Out of 1 million transactions, only about 500 are fraudulent (0.05%). Your baseline model achieves 99.95% accuracy by simply predicting "not fraud" for every transaction — but it misses every single fraud case.

How would you approach this problem? Walk me through your strategy for handling the class imbalance and building a model that can actually detect fraud.

Me

I don't think accuracy is a suitable metric for this case. Since when predicting the fraud, false negative matters more than false positive. I can use a stratified train-test split. And then I will compare methods like class weighting and under/over-sampling on the training set only. I will also tune the classification threshold to balance missed frauds and false alarms base on business needs. Finally I'd monitor model performance and retrain model if necessary.

SchoolAI

You're thinking about this the right way. Accuracy is indeed misleading here — 99.95% accuracy while catching zero fraud is a classic trap.

On techniques: You mentioned comparing class weighting vs. under/over-sampling. How would you actually choose between these approaches? What would make you pick one over the other?

On threshold tuning: You mentioned balancing missed frauds and false alarms based on business needs. How would you operationalize that? What would you ask the business stakeholders to determine the right trade-off?

Precision, recall, F-1 score are more important metrics. If missing a fraud transaction is costly, then recall is most important. If false positive alarms is expensive to investigate or hurt business reputation, then precision matters more. If both important, then we can use F1 score to balance.

Exactly right — you're thinking about the business context, which is crucial. Precision, recall, and F1 are the right tools here, and you've correctly mapped them to the trade-offs.

Scenario 3: Data Drift and Concept Drift

You've deployed a recommendation system for an e-commerce platform that suggests products to users based on their browsing and purchase history. The model was trained on 6 months of historical data and performed well during validation.

Three months after deployment, you notice that the click-through rate (CTR) on recommendations has dropped from 4.2% to 2.8%. The model hasn't changed, but user behavior might have.

How would you diagnose whether this is due to data drift (the distribution of input features has changed) or concept drift (the relationship between features and the target has changed)? Walk me through your investigation strategy and how you'd determine the appropriate response.

Me

I can compare the current feature distribution with training data to detect data drift. If input distribution remains similar but model's prediction accuracy or CTR drops. it's possibly concept drift which means user preference has changed. I will monitor both feature distribution and model performance over time. If I detect data drift I will retrain model with more recent data. If concept drift, I will review the features and potentially redesign the model.

SchoolAI

Good high-level framework. You're distinguishing correctly between the two types of drift and have appropriate response strategies.

Now let's dig into the diagnosis — how would you actually implement this?

For detecting data drift: You mentioned comparing current feature distributions with training data. How would you do that in practice? What statistical tests or metrics would you use? Would you look at all features individually, or some aggregate measure?

For detecting concept drift: You said if input distribution is similar but model performance drops, it's concept drift. How would you measure that drop? What would you compare — CTR on recommendations? Prediction accuracy on held-out recent data? Something else?

Pick one — data drift detection or concept drift detection — and walk me through the concrete implementation.

Me

For detecting data drift, I can compare recent production data with a baseline training window. I may use metrics such as K-S test, or population stability index. I can also compare category frequencies using K-square test or J-S divergence. I will check important features individually and create an overall drift dashboard showing the features exceeding predefined thresholds.

SchoolAI

Solid — you're naming the right statistical tools. KS test for continuous features, chi-square for categorical, PSI and JS divergence as alternatives. A drift dashboard with threshold-based alerts is exactly how you'd operationalize this in production.

Now let me push you on the practical implementation:

How would you choose between these tests? When would you use KS vs. PSI vs. JS divergence for a continuous feature? What characteristics of the data or the drift would guide that choice?

And on thresholds: You mentioned "predefined thresholds." How would you actually determine what those thresholds should be? Would you use statistical significance levels (like p < 0.05)? Would you set them based on historical stability? How would you avoid false alarms from minor fluctuations?

Pick one — test selection or threshold determination — and walk me through your decision framework.

Me

For continuous features I would use KS test when I want to detect any distribution change and determine whether it is statistically significant. I would use PSI for production monitoring for magnitude of drift over time. JS divergence is used when I need a symmetric measure of how different two probability distributions are.

SchoolAI

Nice — you're thinking clearly about the strengths of each test. KS test for statistical significance, PSI for monitoring drift magnitude over time (it's particularly good for that because it's bounded and interpretable), JS divergence when you need a symmetric comparison.

You've built a solid approach for detecting data drift. The threshold question is still open — how you'd actually set those values to avoid false alarms — but that's something you'd refine in practice based on your specific data and tolerance for alerts.

Overall for this scenario, you've covered:

  • Having appropriate response strategies (retrain for data drift, review/redesign for concept drift)
