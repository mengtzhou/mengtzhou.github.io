Data Challenge Scenarios: Reasoning Through Real ML Problems

  • AI-Guided Learning

Introduction

This artifact is an AI-guided problem-solving session for AIML-500 Machine Learning Fundamentals. Instead of reading about data problems in the abstract, I worked one-on-one with a pre-trained chatbot coach. It handed me three realistic scenarios and asked me to think out loud. For each one I laid out how I would approach it, and the coach pushed back with harder questions until I had defended every decision. The exercise rewarded clear reasoning, not a single correct answer.

Objective

The goal was to practice diagnosing the messy data problems that show up constantly in real machine learning work. Three scenarios moved from straightforward to hard: missing data in a churn model, fraud detection on a badly imbalanced dataset, and telling data drift apart from concept drift in a live recommendation system. Each one made me choose metrics, weigh trade-offs, and think about what happens after a model ships.

Project Overview

The coach walked me through the three scenarios in sequence, each set in a concrete business context. I treated every scenario as an open conversation rather than a quiz. When I had glossed over a detail, I doubled back, for example to compare which imputation method actually fits a skewed count feature. The full exchange with the Data Challenge Scenarios Coach is preserved on a separate page.

Process

This was an individual, dialogue-based activity. I worked through the following steps:

  • Reviewed the assignment rubric and the framing for each data challenge scenario.
  • Joined the pre-trained chatbot coach and worked through the three scenarios one at a time.
  • Explained my reasoning for each decision, then answered the coach's follow-up questions on metrics, methods, and trade-offs.
  • Captured the full conversation on a transcript page and synthesized (by Claude [Opus 4.8]) the takeaways into this artifact.

Value Proposition

This artifact shows that I can reason through open-ended data problems, not just recite definitions. In each scenario I picked metrics that matched the business cost, weighed competing techniques against each other, and planned for what happens once a model is live. That judgment separates someone who can run a model from someone who can own one in production.

For employers and collaborators in software, data, and AI roles, this shows practical fluency with the everyday realities of machine learning: dirty data, skewed classes, and models that quietly degrade over time. It also shows that I use an AI coach to sharpen my thinking, not to hand me answers.

Key Learnings

The clearest theme across all three scenarios: the technique matters less than understanding why the data behaves the way it does. Missing values can be a signal worth keeping. A high accuracy number can hide a useless model. A drop in performance can come from two different kinds of drift that need different fixes. Naming the right cause is what points you to the right response.

I also built a stronger habit of tying decisions back to business cost and to life after deployment. I set the fraud threshold by weighing a $500 missed fraud against a $20 false alarm. I set up monitoring so a stale feature triggers a retrain. That framing made the work feel less like a homework exercise and more like the ongoing job of keeping a system honest.
